Introduction

Understanding and serving customers are key strategic objectives in
retail marketing. By analyzing customer data, businesses can enhance
satisfaction and retention through price optimization and targeted
resource allocation.

Customer segmentation enables businesses to group customers by shared
characteristics such as demographics, geography, purchasing behavior,
and preferences.

In this project, we applied machine learning and data-driven
methodologies to develop clustering models for customer segmentation,
aiming to optimize marketing strategies and improve business outcomes.

Objectives

The main goal of this mini-project is to help us understand how to apply
clustering techniques for customer segmentation analysis.

-   Perform EDA for initial understanding of the data set.
-   Preprocess the data and conduct feature engineering
-   Determine the optimal number of clusters (k)
-   Apply ML models to reduce dimensions and segment customers.
-   Write a report with critical findings and recommendations.

Methods

The e-commerce dataset used for customer segmentation was obtained from
this (url), containing 951,668 transaction records from 1 January 2012
to 30 December 2016. Analysis was conducted in Python 3.12.0 using
standard libraries (pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, Seaborn) and
scikit-learn’s clustering algorithms (k-means, hierarchical clustering)
with standard and robust scaling. Clustering quality was assessed via
elbow analysis and silhouette scores, while dimensionality reduction
(PCA) and t-SNE projections were applied for visualization.

Feature engineering focused on five key metrics: purchase frequency,
recency, customer lifetime value (CLV), average unit cost, and customer
age (Table 1). Exploratory data analysis (EDA) verified data quality,
distributions, and outliers. Due to computational constraints,
hierarchical clustering was applied to a subset, while other analyses
used the full dataset. The complete implementation is available in this
(notebook).

Results

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) revealed significant deviations from
normality across all engineered features (see Notebook for detailed
visualizations). This characteristic indicates that careful selection
and interpretation of clustering methods should be exercised, as
distance-based algorithms may be sensitive to these patterns.

Transactional features (frequency, Recency, clv, Avg_Unit_Cost)
contained extreme values beyond typical ranges, initially suggesting
outliers (Figure 1). However, the IQR method flagged no outliers in the
these features (Table 2), indicating that these extreme values may still
represent natural variation rather than anomalies, though domain
knowledge would be needed to confirm this.

After feature engineering, 2,908 missing values (4.3% of 68,300 total
observations) were identified, which were removed to maintain data
integrity for clustering analysis.

[Distribution of Engineered Feature Values]

Comparative analysis of k-means and hierarchical clustering revealed
differences in optimal cluster numbers. Hierarchical clustering produced
variable results (2-6 clusters) depending on linkage methods and scaling
approaches, while k-means with elbow analysis consistently indicated 4-6
clusters (see Notebook). To examine whether extreme values influenced
these discrepancies, unscaled, robust-scaled, and standard-scaled data
was evaluated.

Notably, all scaling methods generated similar elbow plots but showed
degraded silhouette scores, including inconsistent and negative values,
suggesting suboptimal cluster separation. The unscaled data suggested
k=4 as the most stable solution, whereas scaled versions weakly
converged at k=6 (Figure 2, Notebook).

These findings recommend cautious interpretation of the clustering
results. While the methodological consistency between k-means and
unscaled data supports k=4 as a plausible solution, the poor silhouette
scores across all approaches indicate the data may not partition cleanly
into distinct clusters. This could reflect either inherent overlap
between customer segments or limitations of the chosen algorithms in
capturing the data’s underlying structure. Further investigation using
alternative methods might help clarify these results.

To better understand the underlying structure of the data, we performed
visualizations both before and after dimensionality reduction. The
PCA-enhanced t-SNE approach demonstrated superior performance compared
to t-SNE alone, highlighting PCA’s effectiveness in preserving
meaningful patterns for nonlinear projection. While both standard and
robust scaling methods maintained the global data structure in PCA
space, standard scaling yielded slightly more compact k-means clusters
with reduced overlap (see Notebook).

The robustness of the cluster structure was further confirmed by the
strong similarity between k-means and hierarchical clustering results
when visualized through t-SNE with PCA and standard scaling (Figure 3).
This consistency reinforces the validity of k-means with k=4 as an
appropriate method for the analysis of this data set.

[t-SNE-PCA projection k-means versus hierarchical clustering]

Overall, the combination of k-means clustering computational advantages
plus the results discussed above, point towards k-means clustering with
k=4 as the optimal balance between interpretability and methodological
consistency required to performed customer segmentation with this data
set.

The t-SNE-PCA projection of selected features reveals that k-means
clustering captures distinct data structures, which can be interpreted
as meaningful customer behaviors. The clusters align with specific
customer segments—particularly in terms of CLV (Customer Lifetime Value)
and age groups—providing actionable insights into how different
demographics consistently contribute to business revenue.

Conclusions

The lack of normality suggests that distance-based clustering algorithms
(like k-means) may be sensitive to feature scaling and outliers

Hierarchical clustering may provide more robust results given the data
distribution These characteristics should guide both preprocessing
decisions (e.g., robust scaling) and algorithm selection for optimal
segmentation results.
